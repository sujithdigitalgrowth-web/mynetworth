What Is a Merchant Category Code (MCC)? A Simple Guide

By WorthScale Research Desk · Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

Ever wondered why your credit card gives you 5% cashback at some stores but nothing at others — even when you're buying similar things? The answer is usually a four-digit number you never see: the Merchant Category Code, or MCC.

What Is an MCC?

A Merchant Category Code is a four-digit number assigned to a business by its payment processor or acquiring bank, describing what type of business it is — restaurant, grocery store, airline, education institution, and so on. Every time you swipe a card or complete an online payment, this code travels along with the transaction, telling your card issuer what kind of purchase just happened.

You, as the customer, never choose or see this code directly — it's determined by how the merchant is registered with their payment processor, based on their primary business activity.

Why MCCs Matter to You as a Cardholder

  1. Reward rates depend on it. Many credit cards offer bonus rewards on specific categories — say, 5x points on dining, or 3% cashback on groceries. The card network identifies "dining" or "groceries" by checking the MCC attached to each transaction. If a restaurant is coded under a different category for some reason, you might not get the bonus rate you expected, even though you clearly ate at a restaurant.
  2. Some categories are excluded from rewards entirely. Certain transaction types — commonly things like government payments, wallet top-ups, or specific fee categories — are often excluded from earning rewards on many cards, precisely because their MCC is flagged differently from standard retail spending. This is a common source of confusion when a cardholder expects rewards on a bill payment and doesn't receive them.
  3. MCCs can affect fraud monitoring. Card issuers build risk models based partly on typical spending patterns for each MCC. A category where charges are normally occasional and large will be monitored differently from one where frequent small charges are expected. This is a legitimate, standard part of how banks protect cardholders from unauthorized use — not something specific to any one merchant type.
  4. MCCs can affect fee structure for the merchant — which sometimes shows up indirectly in what you pay. Payment processors often price their fees differently by MCC, since risk and processing costs vary by industry. This is primarily a business-side consideration, but it's part of why the "backend" of a card payment is more complex than the simple percentage rate advertised.

How to Find Out a Merchant's MCC

If you're curious why a specific transaction didn't earn rewards, most banks will tell you the MCC used for that transaction if you contact customer support and ask directly — this is the most reliable way to understand a specific case, rather than guessing based on the business type.

A Quick Real-World Example

Imagine paying a coaching center for exam preparation classes. Depending on how that coaching center is registered with its payment processor, the transaction might be coded as "Education Services," "Business Services," or even a general retail code — and each of these can carry different reward eligibility and monitoring rules on your card, even though from your perspective, you're just paying for the same class either way. This is decided entirely by the merchant's registration, not by anything you do at checkout.

A Note on "Cheaper" Categories

Some merchant categories advertise lower headline processing rates than others — but the advertised rate is rarely the full picture. A good way to see this is to compare two very different categories: Education payments and Freelance/Professional Services payments.

Education is often quoted with a lower headline commission percentage — processors tend to treat it as a lower-risk, socially preferred category, the same way the coaching-center example above gets coded. But that lower headline number frequently doesn't include everything the bank adds on afterward: a fixed processing charge, GST on that fee, or a "convenience fee" that shows up separately on the statement instead of inside the advertised rate.

Freelance and professional-services payments usually go the other way. The headline commission percentage quoted looks higher — but it's more often the real, all-in number, with far fewer bank-side charges layered on top afterward.

Add up the true final cost on both sides, and a "cheaper" education payment and a "costlier-looking" freelance payment can land in a similar range by the end. The headline percentage alone doesn't tell you much — what matters is the fully loaded cost once every hidden add-on is counted. If you're evaluating payment categories for a business, it's worth asking your payment provider for a genuine all-in cost estimate rather than comparing headline rates alone.

The Bigger Picture

MCCs exist to help the entire payment ecosystem — banks, processors, and card networks — categorize the enormous volume of transactions happening every day, for purposes ranging from rewards programs to tax reporting to fraud prevention. As a consumer, understanding that this system exists helps explain some of the more confusing moments on a credit card statement — like why a bonus category didn't apply, or why a particular type of payment felt scrutinized more closely than usual.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I choose which MCC a merchant uses?

No. The MCC is determined by the merchant's registration with their payment processor, based on their primary business activity — customers have no control over it.

Why didn't I get reward points on a transaction I expected to earn on?

This usually happens when the MCC assigned to that merchant doesn't match the reward category you expected — for example, a business that seems like a "restaurant" to you might be coded differently by its processor.

Is it normal for banks to monitor certain MCCs more closely?

Yes. Banks build fraud detection models that account for typical spending patterns per category, which is a standard, legitimate part of protecting cardholders — not something unique to any particular merchant type.

Where can I check what MCC a merchant used for my transaction?

Contact your card issuer's customer support directly and ask about the specific transaction — this is the most reliable way to get an accurate answer.
